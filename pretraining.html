How a large language model learns: pretraining

1One simple task: guess the next word

Pretraining teaches a model one thing: read some text and guess what comes next. That is all. Everything ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can do starts from this one exercise, repeated trillions of times.

Your turn: what comes next?

The probabilities are illustrative: they show the kind of answer a well-trained model gives, not the output of a specific model.

Notice two things. First, the model never gives a single answer: it gives a probability for every possible next word. Second, some sentences have an obvious continuation and others do not. No model can know your favourite fruit.

2The text is its own answer key

To learn, a model needs exercises with correct answers. In most of machine learning, humans must write those answers (for example, by labelling photos). Pretraining needs no human labels: every piece of text already contains the answers, because the next word is simply the next word.

One sentence becomes many exercises

Navy = what the model can see. Red = the word it must guess. Then the window moves one step to the right.

Models read tokens, not words

Text is cut into small pieces called tokens: common words, parts of words, digits and punctuation.

Unemployment rose to 4.7% in the UK.

Illustrative split. Each model family uses its own tokenizer. In English, one token is roughly three-quarters of a word. On this page we say “word” to keep things simple.

Economics lens

Labels are free, so data can be huge

Human labelling has a high marginal cost per example. Self-supervised learning has almost zero marginal cost per exercise. This is why pretraining can use trillions of tokens from web pages, books, articles and code. The binding constraints become the amount of good text and the cost of computing power.

3Learning means becoming less surprised

After each guess, we check how much probability the model gave to the word that really came next. If it gave the true word a low probability, the model was surprised. Training adjusts the model so that it is less surprised next time.

Surprise in bits: each halving of the probability adds one bit

p = 10 bitsno surprise
p = 1/21 bitcoin flip
p = 1/83 bits
p = 1/646 bits
p = 1/102410 bitsvery surprised

The training loss is the average surprise over all the text:

Loss = average of  −log2 P( true next word | the words before it )

This measure is called cross-entropy. People also call it log loss.

You already know this

Pretraining is maximum likelihood estimation

In econometrics you choose parameters that make the observed data most likely. Pretraining does the same: it chooses the model’s parameters to make the observed text most likely. Minimising average surprise is exactly the same as maximising the log-likelihood. The differences are scale (billions of parameters) and a very flexible functional form.

How the parameters change

A real model is a neural network called a transformer. Think of it as a very flexible function with billions of adjustable dials (parameters).

After each exercise, the training algorithm (gradient descent) turns every dial a tiny amount in the direction that would have given the true word a higher probability. Repeat this trillions of times.

How is this toy different from a real LLM?

The toy sees only one previous word. It cannot know whether “prices rise” is about demand or supply, because it has already forgotten the start of the sentence. That is why its sentences wander. A real LLM looks back over thousands or even hundreds of thousands of tokens.

The toy has about 5,000 dials and learns from 181 exercises, which it sees over and over. A real model has billions of dials and reads trillions of tokens. The learning rule is the same idea: nudge the dials to reduce surprise.

The toy can never reach zero surprise. The data itself is uncertain: in the training sentences, the word “rates” is followed by “to”, “reduce”, “are” or the end of the sentence. The red line shows the lowest surprise any one-word-memory model can reach on these sentences.

5Why guessing words teaches so much

To guess the next word well across trillions of different documents, a model must pick up the patterns that produced the text. Lower surprise on the next word requires:

Grammar

“She ___ to work by bus.”

→ goes, went, travels

Facts

“The capital of Japan is ___”

→ Tokyo

Economic reasoning

“A tax on sugar will make fizzy drinks ___”

→ more expensive

Arithmetic

“17 × 3 = ___”

→ 51

Keeping track

“Anna lent Ben her umbrella. The next day Ben gave it back to ___”

→ Anna

Style and intent

“Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to ___”

→ apply, complain, enquire

A caution

The model learns the patterns in its text, not the truth. If the training text contains errors, outdated facts or biases, the model learns those too. When a fact is rare in the data, the model can produce a fluent but wrong answer.

6Scale: the inputs to the production function

Pretraining has three main inputs: the number of parameters (N), the amount of training text in tokens (D) and the computing power (C) needed to process it. A widely used rule of thumb links them:

Training compute ≈ 6 × N × D  operations

Scaling laws

Researchers found that the loss falls smoothly and predictably as N, D and C grow. The relation follows a power law. This predictability gives firms the confidence to invest very large sums in bigger training runs.

The Chinchilla study found that, for a fixed compute budget, parameters and data should grow together. Its rule of thumb is about 20 training tokens per parameter.

Economics lens

A huge fixed cost

Pretraining is a large, one-off fixed cost. After that, the marginal cost of answering one more question is small. High fixed costs and low marginal costs have consequences for market structure, pricing and competition. We return to this in later weeks, including the economics of AI infrastructure.

7The result: a base model, not yet an assistant

Pretraining produces a base model: an excellent document-continuer. It continues text in the way the internet would. It does not yet follow instructions or try to be helpful.

A base model might continue:

What comes next: post-training

A second stage, post-training, turns the base model into an assistant. It uses example conversations written by people and reinforcement learning from feedback. This stage teaches the model to answer questions, follow instructions and refuse some requests.

Post-training is the next part of today’s lecture.

Recap

  1. One task. Pretraining teaches a model to give probabilities for the next token.
  2. Free answers. The text itself contains the correct answers, so no human labels are needed and the data can be enormous.
  3. Learning is estimation. Training turns billions of dials to reduce average surprise. This is maximum likelihood estimation.
  4. Prediction needs knowledge. To predict well, the model must absorb grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning, and also the errors and biases in its text.
  5. Scale matters. Loss falls predictably as parameters, data and compute grow.
  6. Base model. The result continues documents. Post-training makes it an assistant.

Check your understanding

Why can the loss never reach zero, even for a perfect model?

Text is uncertain. After “My favourite fruit is”, many words are reasonable. Even the best possible model must spread probability over them, so it is sometimes surprised. In the toy model, the red line shows this floor.

Why do people call pretraining “self-supervised”?

The correct answers (the next tokens) come from the data itself. Nobody needs to label anything, so the supply of training exercises is limited only by the amount of usable text.

The toy model sees only one previous word. What goes wrong, and how do real models fix it?

It cannot use earlier context, so it cannot tell, for example, whether a price change comes from demand or supply. Its sentences drift. Real LLMs use the transformer architecture to look back over very long contexts.

A firm doubles its compute budget. According to the Chinchilla result, how should it spend it?

It should increase both the model size and the amount of training data, roughly in proportion, rather than only making the model bigger.
